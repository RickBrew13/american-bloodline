# Richard Lawrence Shamblen — B-26 Tail Gunner, Korean War (2026-10-19)

Military combat records from the Korean War are highly specific — and many files were lost in the 1973 National Archives fire in St. Louis — making a digital "paper trail" for a survivor's crash landing difficult. But the historical records of the USAF 3rd Bombardment Wing and the Douglas B-26 Invader's design piece together exactly what Richard Lawrence Shamblen (b. February 4, 1932, West Virginia) went through. [1](https://www.werelate.org/wiki/Family:Arvel_Shamblen_and_Georgia_Fisher_(1))

## 1. The Tail Gunner's "Solitary Confinement"

In the Korean War B-26 Invader, the tail gunner did not sit with the pilot and navigator. He was in a completely isolated compartment at the extreme rear of the fuselage, beneath the vertical tail fin.

- **The Position:** Richard crawled through a small hatch under the tail. Inside: a small bicycle-style seat facing backward into the night, cut off from the crew by a bulkhead.
- **The Danger:** Total isolation meant the most hazardous position on the aircraft. If the plane took AAA fire or hit the ground and the fuselage twisted, the hatch could jam — trapping the gunner inside.

## 2. The Nightmare of Korean Night Missions

The 3rd Bombardment Wing specialized in night intruder missions — taking off in darkness from K-8 (Kunsan) or K-14 (Kimpo) to hunt North Korean/Chinese supply trucks along mountainous "Main Supply Routes."

- **Anti-Aircraft Fire:** Supply lines were defended with rapid-fire automatic weapons and heavy AAA. B-26s flew just hundreds of feet above jagged peaks — easy targets.
- **The Crash Landing:** A hit to engines or controls at low altitude gave the pilot seconds. Forced landings on unlit rocky riverbeds or rough emergency fields. For a backward-facing tail gunner: bracing blind for impact, feeling the aircraft tear apart unseen. Surviving — let alone escaping the wreckage — was a miracle.

## 3. The Battlefield Enemy: Malaria

Rice paddies around airbases and low Korean valleys bred Anopheles mosquitoes. Despite bitter chloroquine preventive tablets, tens of thousands of US personnel contracted severe recurring malaria — violent fevers, long field-hospital stays, or evacuation to Japan hospitals.

## How to Get the Official Government Record

Richard survived, came home (police officer, family man), and passed October 3, 1968, in Cincinnati — so he won't appear on casualty lists. To get the actual crash report: [1](https://www.werelate.org/wiki/Family:Arvel_Shamblen_and_Georgia_Fisher_(1))

1. **Request his OMPF:** Through the National Archives eVetRecs portal. As a direct descendant, request his complete DD-214 and service record.
2. **Find the Mishap Report:** The Air Force kept "Mishap Reports"/"Aviation Accident Reports" for every crash-landed plane. Once his squadron/group number is known from the personnel file, request the accident report for his tail number from the Air Force Historical Research Agency (AFHRA).
